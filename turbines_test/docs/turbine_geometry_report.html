1. Executive Summary

This report documents the geometric modifications and the driving algorithmic logic behind the coupled aero-thermal-structural optimization of a high-pressure gas turbine rotor blade. The baseline solid geometry was parameterized along 24 spanwise slices and optimized using a gradient-based multi-agent swarm framework. Over 80 optimization epochs, the algorithms resolved competing trade-offs between component mass, aerodynamic flow deflection, and conjugate heat transfer margins. The final design yields a 22.23% mass reduction, a 6.9% increase in aerodynamic efficiency (L/D), and holds surface temperatures below critical thermal creep boundaries.

2. Spanwise Geometric Modifications

The optimization algorithm modified two primary profiles along the 120mm blade height: the external shape scale factor profile $w(z)$ (which scales chord length and thickness in 2D) and the internal relative lattice density profile $d(z)$ (which governs internal cooling structure volume fraction). The resulting geometry changes are highly non-uniform and can be divided into three zones:

Figure 2.1: Airfoil profile cross-sectional comparison before (dashed gray) and after (solid dark) optimization, showing internal cooling core boundaries (green).

2.1 The Root Zone (Z = 0mm to 30mm)

The external geometry at the root was scaled up by 15% to 20% ($w \approx 1.20$), resulting in a thickened airfoil neck and root fillet. Concurrently, the internal lattice density converged to its maximum limit ($d = 1.0$), creating a fully solid core. This local enlargement increases the nominal cross-sectional area, directly countering the massive mechanical stress concentration induced by centrifugal force.

2.2 The Mid-Span Zone (Z = 30mm to 75mm)

The mid-span geometry underwent a tapering process, with the external scale factor reduced by 10% to 15% ($w \approx 0.85$). In this region, the internal lattice structure was activated, settling at a relative density of 60% to 70%. This represents a hollowed core that extracts heat efficiently due to high-surface-area internal channels, while retaining bending stiffness.

2.3 The Tip Zone (Z = 75mm to 120mm)

The blade tip was aggressively thinned, with the scale factor dropping to its lower bound of 0.80. The internal density was minimized to the physical boundary limit of 45% to 50% ($d \approx 0.45$), forming a thin-walled, hollow tip shell. Since tip mass creates the highest centrifugal load on the root, removing material here yields the highest cascading stress relief down the blade span.

3. Algorithmic Optimization Logic

The multi-agent swarm utilizes a coupled cost function mapping the interactions between flow parameters, thermal transport, and structural stress. The shape of the blade was evolved by balancing four physical gradients:

Loss = w_mass * J_mass + w_aero * J_aero + w_thermal * J_thermal + Penalty_stress
  1. Mass Reduction Gradient: Drives both external scale $w_i$ and internal density $d_i$ downward to minimize overall volume and centrifugal load. The mass scales non-linearly: area scales quadratically ($w_i^2$) with external dimensions and linearly ($d_i$) with internal density.
  2. Aerodynamic Lift-to-Drag Gradient: Maximizes flow efficiency ($\eta_{avg}$). The algorithm drives the external scale $w_i$ downward to minimize profile drag (which is proportional to the square of thickness: $C_D \propto w_i^2$). This matches aerodynamic theory where a thinner blade profile cuts drag and delays flow separation.
  3. Conjugate Heat Transfer (CHT) Gradient: Regulates temperature limits ($T_max \le 900^\circ\text{C}$). The algorithm balances the external size and density to increase internal cooling capacity:
    h_coolant ∝ sqrt(d_i) • (1/w_i)^0.3
    If the blade gets too hot, the algorithm increases internal density $d_i$ (increasing cooling surface area) or scales down the external shape $w_i$ to reduce the gas contact area.
  4. Structural Stress Boundary: The safety factor constraint forces the root area to expand. If centrifugal or thermal stresses exceed yield margins, the structural critic applies heavy mathematical penalties, overriding mass-saving objectives at the root and expanding the neck.
